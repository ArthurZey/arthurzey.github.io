## The Murray Backstory

When [I bought my home in Bailey in 2021](/blog/2022-01-01/2021-retrospective/#buying-my-first-home), it [came with a cat](/blog/2021-11-06/apparently-i-have-a-cat/). Murray was a good boy. Sometimes annoying, but as far as cats go, one of the good ones.

The last time I laid eyes on him was in winter of 2023, when he had gotten very chonky. We suspect that a neighbor had started feeding him. Either that, or Murray ate a fox. Seriously, he was a total badass, and I have seen him chase foxen away from the front yard.

But the chonk made him less agile, and we suspect that made him less resilient to avoiding predators. I caught him on camera in May 2023 and attempted to secure him with an [animal trap](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B079L52MKG){:target="&lowbar;blank"}, so that I could bring him inside and care for him until he could get healthier, but we only ever caught a skunk (which was a disaster, but a story for another time).

Meanwhile, we started [posting](https://nextdoor.com/p/z-Z4gmMs3Tpz){:target="&lowbar;blank"} everywhere we could think of to see if anybody found him. At some point, someone messaged me on Nextdoor to alert me to [another neighbor's post about a found cat that looked exactly like Murray](https://nextdoor.com/p/JSsXBMMhxzSM){:target="&lowbar;blank"}.

## Finding Murray?

I immediately drove 12 miles to the neighboring town (8 miles as the crow flies through the mountain wilderness). They found the cat under their porch and had him confined to their laundry room because they had a dog and weren't equipped to care for a cat. The cat looked almost identical to Murray, down to his mannerisms, but was small and skinny. I attributed that to his 8-mile adventure. I was unable to be objective in that moment.

I took the cat, and on the drive home, I called Chase. I just suddenly knew: "It isn't Murray."

We took this cat to the vet to verify that he didn't have Murray's microchip--indeed, he had no chip.

And then I noticed a subtle difference in coloring on one of his white "socks".

Anyway, we posted everywhere we could think of to try to find the original family, without any luck.

We knew that he had come from a home: He knew that there were interesting things in the refrigerator, he knew what the sound of a shaking bag of kibble meant, he had no fear of dogs, and he immediately knew how to use a litterbox.

I was calling him "Faux Murray", but once it became obvious that we were keeping this cat, Melissa had the great idea to call him "Murrby". (Get it? "Murray", but with a "b", because he was Murray's successor.) I joked that if we got another cat after Murrby, we'd have to name her "Mercy" or "Murrcy".

## Our First Scare

At some point, I found Murrby dripping wet. Not long after, in another episode, I noticed him frothing at the mouth after spinning around, out of control. At first, I worried that he might be rabid, but there's no practical way to test for that in a live animal.

I took him to the vet and described the episodes. They concluded that he was epileptic and prescribed twice-daily phenobarbital pills to control his seizures and midazolam (which acts as an anticonvulsant) to squirt into his mouth with a syringe in case of an actual seizure.

For the next few years, the frequency of his seizures decreased to the point that, as of today exactly, we haven't observed one in two years.

When he had a seizure, it was terrifying, heartbreaking, and dangerous. He would let out a horrific howl, lose control of his bladder, and then start spinning around in circles, with no control of his claws. Catching him and holding him down to administer the midazolam has left me with several physical scars. But it seemed to help, the seizures would stop, and he'd stumble around like an idiot for a few minutes, then eventually return to normal.

## An Otherwise Good Cat

It took a while for Murrby to find his "voice" in terms of purring--he was no Murray--but he was very affectionate. It was nice to have him around, and his fearlessness with Max (and later, Thor) didn't trigger their prey drive, leading to happy coexistence with the dogs. I never would have thought that Max could be chill around a cat.

Murrby was also a terrible jumper. We joked that he didn't know how to "cat" very well. He couldn't chase or catch anything for shit. So he didn't help with a mouse problem that we had. But he also didn't go up on counters, which was great, because whatever stereotypes there are about cats' cleanliness, cats prance around in litterboxes full of piss and feces, and I do not want that on my counters. We suspect that his bad "catting" had to do with brain damage from the seizures. Chase would often describe him as not having two brain cells to rub together. Truly, Murrby is a derp.

Unlike his predecessor, Murray, Murrby would not last 15 minutes outdoors.

## Peeing in Unfortunate Places

And then we discovered that Murrby had been peeing in the foyer. We finally landed on [an enzyme spray](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00MOOYQP0){:target="&lowbar;blank"} that did a pretty good job of neutralizing the smell of cat piss--to which I'm extremely sensitive--but no amount of [deterrent sprays](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09Y2HCBPQ){:target="&lowbar;blank"} or even a baby gate (which he managed to squeeze under) could stop him from getting to the foyer.

We even took him with us when we went to Kentucky for a few months in December 2025 for Dax's birth and then to our temporary apartment in Denver for 6 months. He managed to find spots on the hardwood floor and carpet to pee on. Cleaning took a lot of effort. And it was infuriating.

We knew that he knew how to use a litterbox. After all, he still pooped there. And he knew the litterbox was related to peeing, because he would often pee immediately outside it.

When we returned home to Bailey in August 2026, we confined him to the utility room to validate that he could reliably use his litterbox. He did. So we expanded his territory to the master suite. And then he started peeing on the floor right outside his litterbox in the bathroom. And then he decided he was going to pee on the floor in the bedroom. Cleaning up his piss became a daily chore.

We have tried everything: (obviously) scooping his litterbox promptly, using a [litter attractant](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000PKUUU0){:target="&lowbar;blank"}, elevating his litterbox, [pheromone-based calming collars](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BLKW38T3){:target="&lowbar;blank"}, adorning our home with feline accoutrements like [spike mats](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0B1DHLN27){:target="&lowbar;blank"}, making sure he had privacy (including an [enclosed litterbox](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0823GN4VR){:target="&lowbar;blank"}), trying a different type of litter (he rejected it), praise / positive reinforcement, punishment, numerous veterinary consultations to rule out medical issues, and more.

We suspected that the phenobarbital could be contributing to the urinary behavior, but his patterns didn't seem consistent with simply losing control of his bladder or failing to reach the litterbox in time (the documented side-effects of the drug).

What has become especially maddening is that he seems to develop preferences for particular places. He knows where his litterbox is, and he'll use it sometimes. Other times, he'll pee right next to it, including when we're standing there.

Whether because he's brain-damaged, irresistibly drawn to peeing in certain places, or just being a vindictive jerk (perhaps for being confined to the master suite), his behavior seems intentional. But that's neither here nor there.

Things have gotten dramatically worse since September.

As of November 2026, approximately 9 out of 10 urinations are happening outside his litterbox.

Nine. Out. Of. Ten.

So now Murrby is confined to our bathroom. It's the only way to contain the damage. But the bathroom constantly smells of cat piss, and every time I go in there, I'm bracing myself for another puddle.

I find the odor intensely dysregulating. It's not merely an inconvenience. It's a source of acute frustration and anger, day after day.

And what kind of life is that for Murrby? A cat confined to a bathroom, cared for by people who increasingly resent his presence?

## Another Medical Problem

During Murrby's annual veterinary examination on 2026-09-22, Crow Hill Veterinary Hospital discovered a painful dental resorptive lesion affecting tooth 207. More recently, I looked in his mouth myself and could see blood and inflammation around the affected tooth.

He clearly has a dental problem that needs attention.

It occurred to us that perhaps the pain is contributing to his increasingly problematic urinary behavior. Chronic discomfort could plausibly be making things worse.

But the peeing problem started long before the dental issue was diagnosed, and it has persisted through multiple homes and living arrangements. So I'm not particularly optimistic that fixing his tooth will fix his behavior. Even if pain contributed to the recent deterioration, the behavior itself could now be an established habit.

Still, we have to consider the possibility that treating the dental issue might relieve his pain, improve his quality of life, and perhaps even improve his prospects for rehoming.

The problem, of course, is money.

Crow Hill's estimate for dental treatment, including possible extractions, was $1299.45 to $2695.75. That's an enormous range, particularly given that we know of one specifically affected tooth.

Murrby has accident and illness coverage through Figo Pet Insurance. Allegedly, medically necessary dental treatment may be eligible for reimbursement, but Figo has been extraordinarily unhelpful in explaining which expenses it would cover. I've spent weeks trying to get answers about the policy's actual terms, rather than demanding a guarantee of reimbursement, and have mostly received circular explanations about how they can't adjudicate a claim before it's submitted.

We also have to decide whether to renew Murrby's expired veterinary wellness plan and whether the dental upgrade makes financial sense. The catch is that these are annual commitments. If Murrby is rehomed, we'd still need to settle the plan's financial obligations or reverse the discounts.

I'm not willing to spend another $1000 or more out of pocket on him. Full stop.

That doesn't mean I'm unwilling to help him at all. It means that whatever intervention we consider must have a reasonably predictable, limited financial cost.

## Where Things Stand

Today, I reached out to four veterinary organizations:

- Crow Hill Veterinary Hospital, where Murrby is currently treated, to ask for a more specific, itemized estimate, clarification of the wellness-plan options, and a conversation with his primary veterinarian
- Planned Pethood International in Conifer, which advertises lower-cost dental procedures
- Humane Colorado's Veterinary Hospital at CSU Spur in Denver, which also advertises comparatively affordable dental services
- VRCC in Englewood, to inquire about dental surgery and possible referrals to less expensive providers

I explained the severity of our circumstances to all of them. We're seeking financially manageable dental treatment, but we also asked for help identifying potential adopters, foster homes, and rescue organizations.

No treatment has been scheduled yet, and we don't know what the actual final expense would be.

We're also confronting some logistical problems around upcoming holiday travel. We had intended to bring Murrby along for one of the trips, but I'm extremely concerned about foisting his urinary behavior on Chase's dad, who would be caring for him for part of the trip, and about the possibility of his peeing all over an Airbnb.

We're not rearranging our travel plans because of Murrby. We need to find an arrangement that works within those commitments.

## With a Heavy Heart...

Look--I don't even especially like cats. But I _do_ love animals, and cats qualify.

I loved that Murrby taught Max (and then Thor) how to chill out around other creatures. I imagined that Dax would benefit from being around more different kinds of animals, both from a cognitive-emotional-development perspective and in terms of his microbiome. I urgently wanted to make it work.

[My Anxious-Preoccupied (AP) tendencies](/personality-and-behavioral-profiles/#attachment-style) dispose me to struggling to call it quits--the feeling is adjacent to that which accompanies failure. And that's to say nothing of the tremendous responsibility I feel for a living creature that I have elected to care for and provide a good life and home for.

But I have to be honest with myself: Our challenges with Murrby have led to an unfortunate state of affairs and a vicious cycle. And, whether through my own fault or not, I _have_ failed.

Fundamentally, Murrby has become a burden, and we do not enjoy his company any longer. His presence is a source of irritation. The anxiety of anticipating the overwhelming aroma of cat piss even I enter my bedroom or bathroom is more than I want to bear.

Our lives are not enhanced by "caring" for him. He has become a source of acute frustration, anger, and unbridled fury. And his life is correlatively worse than it could be, for being imprisoned by people who resent his existence.

We have sunk a significant amount of time, money, and emotional energy into this cat. We have had him on veterinary wellness plans, purchased pet insurance, and diligently attended to his medical needs, including securing his prescription medication and consistently pilling him morning and night. We can't go on trips without arranging care with someone willing to administer the phenobarbital.

And meanwhile, I'm taking a break from working so that I can be a full-time dad to our 10.5-month-old son, Dax. I don't have employment income right now, and my time and emotional capacity aren't exactly unlimited.

The amount we've already spent on enzyme cleaners, alternative litterboxes, spike mats, calming collars, etc is absurd and excessive--especially for a cat who came into our lives accidentally and whom, by all rights, I had no obligation to take in in the first place.

Frankly, I am done.

I am done trying endlessly to correct his behavior. I am done trying to divine what is causing it. I am done absorbing an indefinite financial, emotional, and temporal burden.

We're ready to rehome Murrby _now_, if someone is able and willing to take him.

I recognize that this may be extremely difficult. He has epilepsy requiring twice-daily medication, a painful dental issue, and persistent house-soiling behavior. None of those is particularly attractive to a prospective adopter.

If we cannot find an affordable treatment option or a suitable new home, we may need to discuss humane euthanasia with our veterinarian.

I'm not ready to make that decision. However angry, frustrated, and dysregulated I am about the situation, the prospect of ending Murrby's life weighs heavily on my heart.

I'm also acutely aware of the possibility of regret. If there's some treatable medical issue contributing to the recent worsening, I don't want to look back and wonder whether we could have saved his life, or at least made it more comfortable, with one reasonable intervention.

But I cannot justify unlimited additional costs on the mere possibility that something might help.

I've already done far more than I ever anticipated doing for this cat. And I have to draw a line somewhere.

## Can You Help?

I'm publishing this in part because I hope that someone in my extended circle might be able to help us find a better outcome for Murrby.

I'd be particularly grateful for

- someone who might be interested in adopting Murrby, with full knowledge of his epilepsy, medication requirements, dental condition, and inappropriate urination
- referrals to credible rescue organizations, foster homes, or individuals equipped to care for a cat with special needs
- recommendations for affordable veterinary dental treatment, particularly supervised veterinary training programs or clinics that provide comprehensive care at lower cost
- thoughtful advice from people who have navigated similar situations, especially those involving chronic house-soiling or difficult decisions about an animal's quality of life

For those inclined to recommend a new litterbox, different litter, pheromone collars, or cleaning products, I appreciate the intention, but please understand that we've already spent years working through those sorts of interventions. We're not at the beginning of this problem.

If you know someone who might be able to give Murrby a better home, or have a concrete recommendation that could help us find a humane, affordable path forward, please reach out.

I would be enormously grateful.

I'd really like this story to have a better ending.
